🌀 Conicity — How a Rigid Axle Steers Itself

📖 Before you start: the idea in three steps
  1. One axle, one speed. Both wheels are pressed onto the same axle, so they always turn at exactly the same rate (same rpm). Neither wheel can spin faster than the other.
  2. Wheels are cones, not cylinders. The tread is tapered 1:20 — a bigger radius on the flange side, a smaller radius on the outside. If the wheelset sits off-centre, the two wheels touch the rails at different radii.
  3. Bigger radius = longer path. Distance per turn = 2π × radius. The wheel on the larger radius runs further every turn, so the wheelset swings towards the side with the smaller radius. No steering mechanism, no driver input — just geometry.

🌊 Klingel's Formula — Why Trains Sway on Straight Track

📖 Before you start: what is "hunting"?

Conicity steers a wheelset back to the centre — but it arrives at the centre still pointing slightly sideways, so it overshoots to the other side, then corrects again, and again. Like a driver who over-corrects on a slippery road, the wheelset swings left–right–left down the track in a smooth snake-like wave. That is hunting. In 1883 Klingel showed that the length of one full swing is:

L = 2π · √( r₀ · e₀ / λ )
The four sliders — what they mean and what happens when you push them
r₀ wheel radius (0.46 m for a 920 mm wheel). ↑ bigger wheels → longer wavelength, calmer ride.
e₀ half the distance between the two contact points (≈ 0.75 m). ↑ wider contact spacing → longer wavelength.
λ conicity = tan of the taper angle. 1:20 → 0.05. ↑ steeper taper → shorter wavelength, faster swaying. Wear raises it.
Speed doesn't change the wavelength (that's geometry) but sets the frequency: f = speed ÷ L. ↑ faster → more swings per second.

The cards give the real wavelength L, frequency f and cycles per minute. Colour bands: < 1.5 Hz gentle sway · 1.5–3 Hz noticeable · > 3 Hz approaching a bogie's own natural frequencies — this is where yaw dampers earn their keep.

🧮 Traction & Adhesion — How Much Can a Locomotive Pull?

📖 Before you start: what limits a locomotive?

A locomotive pulls through friction between steel wheels and steel rail — a contact patch only about the size of a small coin under each wheel. That friction is called adhesion. The most pulling force the wheels can transmit before they spin is:

Fmax = μ · mdriven · g

Only the weight on the driven axles counts — that is why locomotives are built so heavy. Going uphill, the train needs a force of m · g · (gradient ÷ 1000) just to avoid rolling backwards (1‰ = 1 m of climb per km). If the needed force is above Fmax, the wheels slip.

The inputs — and what to expect when you change them
Driven axles × axle load — total weight pressing on powered wheels. ↑ more driven axles → proportionally more pulling force. (Bo′Bo′ = 4, Co′Co′ = 6.)
μ — rail condition — dry ≈ 0.30, damp ≈ 0.20, wet ≈ 0.15, icy / leaves ≈ 0.10. Wet rail halves your pulling power.
Gradient (‰) — steeper = more force needed. 25‰ is a hard freight grade; high-speed lines are built far gentler.
Train mass — locomotive plus wagons. Double the train, double the force needed.
Reading the result: the green arrow is what the wheels can deliver, the red arrow is what the slope demands. Green longer than red → the train climbs. The tool counts gradient only; in real life rolling, curve and air resistance add to the red arrow, so real margins are thinner.

Part 1 — The Puzzle and the Three Vehicle Families

Here is the question that drives this whole week. A railway wheelset has two wheels pressed rigidly onto one axle, so they always turn at exactly the same rate. In a curve, though, the outer wheel has to travel a longer arc than the inner one. There is no differential, no steering rack, no driver turning anything. So how does a train get round a bend without scrubbing its wheels along the rail?

The answer is built into the shape of the wheel — and we will get there in Part 4. First, the cast of characters. Railway vehicles fall into three families:

🚂
Locomotive

A traction vehicle: it creates the pull and carries no payload. Passenger and freight locomotives are designed differently — speed versus pulling force.

🚃
Wagons & Coaches

Trailed vehicles with no power of their own. Coaches carry passengers, wagons carry freight. A locomotive moves them.

🚄
Multiple Units

Self-propelled train-sets: EMU (electric) or DMU (diesel). Power is spread along the train instead of concentrated in one locomotive.

Why spread the power? With motors under many axles, a multiple unit has lots of driven axles and a light axle load — so it accelerates quickly without hammering the track. That is ideal for commuter lines with a station every couple of kilometres. A locomotive-hauled freight train does the opposite: one heavy machine concentrates weight on its driven wheels (more adhesion) and can be swapped, doubled up or coupled to any length of train. A multiple unit has motor coaches (powered) and trailers (unpowered), coupled permanently or semi-permanently.
Numbers to anchor it: a standard UIC-type passenger coach is about 26.4 m long, with its two bogie pivots about 19 m apart and a bogie wheelbase of about 2.56 m. A common freight-wagon bogie (the Y25 type) has a wheelbase of only 1.8 m. Keep these in mind — they matter for curving in Part 3.

Part 2 — Anatomy of a Vehicle and the Load Path

Every railway vehicle is built from three structural groups:

  • Bodywork (carbody) — the enclosed structure carrying passengers or freight, plus the driver's cab and, in powered vehicles, the traction equipment.
  • Chassis (underframe) — the structural base the carbody sits on. It carries the draw gear (couplers, which transmit pulling and pushing forces between vehicles) and the buffers (spring-loaded pads that cushion impacts), along with lighting and heating systems.
  • Bogies and wheelsets — carry the load from above, deliver it to the track, and allow smooth rolling along the rails.
The sprung / unsprung line. Everything above the axleboxes is sprung — separated from the track by springs and dampers. The wheelset (wheels, axle, bearings, and any motor hung directly on the axle) is unsprung: it follows every rail irregularity directly, with nothing to cushion it.

Why two suspension stages? A spring only isolates vibration at frequencies well above its own natural frequency. The soft secondary suspension (around 1 Hz) filters out the slow sway and the low-frequency components of track roughness, which is what passengers feel. The stiffer primary suspension (a few Hz) has a different job: keep the wheel pressed on the rail over short, sharp irregularities such as joints, welds and switches. Together they let the carbody float while the wheels stay in contact.

Why unsprung mass is the villain. A passenger wheelset typically weighs on the order of 1.3–2 tonnes, and it is that mass — not the carbody's — that smashes into every rail irregularity. The force it generates grows with speed. More unsprung mass means larger dynamic forces on rail, fastenings, sleepers and ballast, and faster track deterioration (Week 2). This is why modern designs mount traction motors on the bogie frame or the carbody instead of hanging them on the axle, and why every line has a maximum axle load.

What is a "line category"? European standard EN 15528 labels every line with a letter and a number — for example D4: the letter gives the permitted axle load (D = 22.5 t) and the number gives the permitted load per metre (4 = 8.0 t/m). Vehicles are matched to lines by these codes. The full table is in the Data tab.

The complete load path from passenger to ground has nine steps: pivot → cross beams → spring group → side frames → axle box → wheel → rail → fastening → sleeper / ballast / subgrade. Use the interactive load path to explore each one.

Part 3 — The Bogie

A bogie is a structural sub-frame that connects two or more wheelsets and does three jobs:

1. Suspension

Isolates the carbody from track irregularities through primary (axle ↔ bogie) and secondary (bogie ↔ carbody) suspension.

2. Guidance

Keeps both wheelsets aligned with the rails and provides lateral stability, with yaw dampers suppressing hunting at speed.

3. Curving

The bogie rotates under the carbody about the pivot. The carbody stays almost straight while each bogie turns to follow the rails.

Structurally the bogie frame is an H: two side frames (longitudinal beams that carry load from the suspension to the axleboxes) joined by transoms (transverse beams that spread load from the pivot to both side frames).

Most bogies have two wheelsets; heavy freight vehicles can have more. A bogie also lets its wheelsets deviate slightly from parallel while curving — and that flexibility is what keeps flange and rail wear down.

Why not just use a long rigid vehicle?

In a curve, an axle that cannot swivel meets the rail at an angle of attack of roughly ψ ≈ wheelbase ÷ (2 × R) (ignoring free play). That angle is what makes flanges grind.

Worked comparison — a 300 m curve
Bogie with a 2.56 m wheelbase: ψ ≈ 2.56 ÷ (2 × 300) = 0.0043 rad ≈ 0.24°
Same 26.4 m coach with just two axles 19 m apart: ψ ≈ 19 ÷ (2 × 300) = 0.032 rad ≈ 1.8° — about 7 times larger.
Short-wheelbase bogies that swivel keep the angle of attack small — that is exactly why the bogie was invented.
The pivot in action: in a curve each bogie yaws to point along the rails while the carbody, held at the two pivots, runs a smooth chord between them. Because the bogies do the curving, a 26 m carbody can take curves with far less flange wear than a rigid vehicle of the same length.

Part 4 — Wheels, Conicity and Hunting

The wheel

A wheel has two functional parts: the tread — the rolling surface that carries the vertical load and provides adhesion — and the flange — the inner guiding rim that prevents derailment and provides lateral guidance as a last resort.

DimensionValueNote
Wheel diameter350–1,250 mm (typically 890–920 mm on main lines)Varies with vehicle type; profiles standardised, e.g. EN 13715
Flange height29 mmAbove the tread
Flange thickness32.5 mmMeasured 10 mm below the tread
Tread taper (conicity)1:20 (tan α = 0.05, α ≈ 2.86°)Some networks use 1:40

Why match the taper on wheel and rail? Because it maximises the contact area, reduces contact stress and wear, and gives smooth, stable running at speed. If the two did not agree, contact would be at an edge — a small area, high stress and rapid wear.

Self-steering, step by step

  1. The wheelset drifts sideways toward the outer rail.
  2. The outer wheel now touches the rail on the wider part of its cone — a larger rolling radius. The inner wheel touches on the narrower part.
  3. Same rpm, larger radius → the outer wheel rolls further per turn.
  4. The wheelset turns towards the curve — with no steering input at all. This is called kinematic steering.
How far must the wheelset shift? Let the curve radius be R, wheel radius r₀, half the distance between contact points e₀, and conicity λ.
• The outer rail is longer than the inner in the ratio (R + e₀) : (R − e₀). So the wheels need radii in the same ratio: Δr ≈ 2·r₀·e₀ / R.
• A sideways shift y changes the two radii by ±λy, so Δr = 2·λ·y.
• Equate: y = r₀·e₀ / (λ·R)
Lateral shift neededy = r₀ · e₀ / (λ · R)
Two worked examples (r₀ = 0.46 m, e₀ = 0.75 m, λ = 0.05; the new-wheel flangeway play is about ±5 mm):
• R = 1,400 m → y = 0.46 × 0.75 ÷ (0.05 × 1,400) = 4.9 mm — just fits inside the play. Conicity alone steers.
• R = 300 m → y = 0.345 ÷ (0.05 × 300) = 23 mm — far more than the 5 mm available. The flange takes over.
So in this simple model conicity alone can steer curves larger than about 1.4 km. Below that, the flange meets the rail: wear, noise, heat. Engineers respond with gauge widening, flange lubrication, speed limits and bogies that help steer — and we will return to this in the Curves lectures. (Real wheel–rail profiles are not perfectly linear cones; this is the idealised model.)

The price of self-steering: hunting

On straight track the same conicity causes trouble. The wheelset steers back to the centre, arrives still pointing sideways, overshoots, corrects, overshoots again — a smooth snake-like swing called hunting. Klingel showed in 1883 that the wavelength is

Klingel (1883)L = 2π √( r₀ · e₀ / λ )
r₀ = 0.46 m · e₀ = 0.75 m · λ = 0.05 → L ≈ 16.5 m

The frequency is simply speed ÷ wavelength: about 1.7 Hz at 100 km/h and about 4.2 Hz at 250 km/h. At low frequency the sway is gentle and dies out. As the frequency climbs towards the natural frequencies of the bogie, the oscillation can grow rather than decay — above this critical speed the vehicle is unstable and, in the worst case, can derail.

The engineering trade-off: more conicity → better curving but a lower critical speed. Less conicity → higher stability but worse curving. The toolkit: yaw dampers between bogie and carbody, tuned bogie stiffness, low-conicity wheel profiles for fast fleets, and regular wheel re-profiling — because wear changes the tread shape and raises the effective conicity.
Common misconceptions.
✗ "The flange steers the train." — On gentle curves the flange never touches the rail; conicity does the steering. The flange is the safety net.
✗ "The outer wheel spins faster." — No. Both wheels turn at the same rpm; the outer one simply rolls on a larger radius.
✗ "Wheels are flat cylinders." — They are shallow cones, 1:20.

What limits a locomotive? Adhesion and power

Two separate limits control how hard a locomotive can pull, and which one bites depends on speed.

1. Adhesion limit (low speed): Fmax = μ · mdriven · g. A Bo′Bo′ locomotive with four 22.5 t axles has 90 t on its driven wheels. Dry rail (μ ≈ 0.30): ≈ 265 kN. Wet rail (μ ≈ 0.15): ≈ 132 kN.
2. Power limit (high speed): F = P / v. A 5 MW locomotive at 100 km/h (27.8 m/s) can deliver at most 5,000 kW ÷ 27.8 m/s ≈ 180 kN; at 200 km/h only ≈ 90 kN.
The two curves cross at v = P ÷ Fmax = 5,000 ÷ 265 ≈ 18.9 m/s ≈ 68 km/h for this example. Below it the locomotive is adhesion-limited (it could use more power but the wheels would slip); above it, it is power-limited.

Gradient. To avoid rolling backwards, a train on a slope needs a force of m·g·(grade ÷ 1000). A 1,000 t train on 25‰ needs about 245 kN (just for gravity). On dry rail the locomotive above delivers 265 kN — it just fits. At 35‰ the train needs about 343 kN and the wheels slip. On wet rail even 15‰ (≈ 147 kN) is too much. The fixes: more driven axles, helper locomotives, sand on the rail — or a gentler route. (This counts gradient only; rolling, curve and air resistance add further force.)

That is why locomotives are heavy. Only the weight on driven wheels creates adhesion, so for a locomotive weight is a feature, not a flaw. It is also why the traction calculator above uses driven axles only.

Read it: only dry rail clears the dashed line (265 kN vs 245 kN). On damp rail (≈ 177 kN) the same locomotive on the same slope slips.
How far to trust the μ values? The formula F = μ · m · g is exact; the μ values are typical orders of magnitude, not constants. Real adhesion falls as speed rises, drops sharply with contamination (leaf film, oil, rust, damp) and varies along the line, so designers use conservative values — and leaf contamination can push adhesion well below 0.10. Sanding the rail and wheel-slip control systems exist for exactly this reason. The chart also counts gravity only; rolling, curve and air resistance add to the force needed.

04DesignYou Are the Fleet Planner▼
