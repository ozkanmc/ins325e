Part 2 — 500 Years of Railway History

The 1,435 mm gauge you will use in every design exercise this term traces directly to mine wagon widths in 1820s northeast England. That is how consequential early engineering choices are.

~1550s
First guided rail transport: horse-drawn wagons on wooden rails in German and English mines. Animals could haul 3–4× more on a smooth guided surface than on rutted ground — a simple insight that launched an industry.
1760
England: wooden mine rails covered with cast iron plates. Load capacity and durability increase dramatically. The concept of the iron rail is born.
1782–1804
James Watt patents continuous rotary steam engine (1782). Richard Trevithick builds the first steam locomotive (1804): transported 10 t iron + 70 people over 15 km in 2 hours. The boiler exploded on the return trip — progress is rarely smooth.
1825
Stockton & Darlington Railway — first public steam railway. Primarily freight. George Stephenson chose 4 ft 8½ in = 1,435 mm based on Tyneside colliery wagon widths — a decision that would govern global railway engineering for two centuries.
1830
Liverpool & Manchester Railway — first inter-urban steam railway for both passengers and freight on a regular timetable. Speeds of 48 km/h. By the 1920s, railways spanned every continent.
1851 (Ottoman)
First railway on Ottoman territory: Alexandria–Cairo, Egypt, 211 km. First within modern Turkish borders: İzmir–Aydın line, 130 km, British concession, completed 1866. By the Republic's founding, 4,136 km of foreign-built railway were nationalised (1924 law).
1923–1950
The Railway Era of the Turkish Republic. ~200 km/year built. By 1950: railways carried 42% of passengers and 55% of freight. Then the Marshall Plan shifted investment to roads — a decision still felt today.
2009–2024
Turkish HSR expansion. Ankara–Eskişehir HSR (2009) was transformative. By end of 2024: 2,032 km of HSR (250 km/h). Total network: 13,919 km. Major ongoing investments — you may be designing parts of this network in your career.

Most common cause (2018–2023): Level crossing collisions = 60–75% of serious accidents every year. This is an infrastructure design and road-user behaviour problem — not an inherent rail deficiency. Grade separation is the engineering solution: as crossings are bridged or tunnelled, accident rates fall toward zero.

In 2023, Turkish highways recorded one accident every 24 seconds. Turkish main-line railways: 9 serious accidents in an entire year. Per billion passenger-km, European railways are ~30× safer than cars.

Part 4 — Railway Geometric Standards

Every railway parameter you will calculate this semester — curve radius, gradient, superelevation, transition length — derives from a set of engineering standards established before a single metre of track is laid.

Six key geometric parameters: Track Gauge · Structure/Clearance Gauge · Horizontal Curves (radius, superelevation, transition) · Vertical Curves (type, radius) · Longitudinal Gradient (‰) · Cross-sections

4.1 — Track Gauge

Track gauge = distance between the inner faces of the two rail heads, measured 14 mm below the running surface (UIC convention). It is the most fundamental dimensional standard. Two trains on different gauges cannot operate on each other's infrastructure without major intervention.

Why 1,435 mm? George Stephenson chose ~4 ft 8½ in for the 1825 Stockton & Darlington Railway — matching existing northeast English colliery wagon widths. British engineers then built railways worldwide in the 1830s–1860s, exporting this gauge globally. It became the UIC international standard. Today used on ~57% of global route-km — not because it is theoretically optimal, but because it got there first. This is path dependence: early engineering choices create locked-in infrastructure that is extremely expensive to change.

4.2 — Structure Gauge (Clearance Envelope)

The structure gauge defines the minimum cross-sectional space that fixed infrastructure must keep clear around the track — tunnels, bridges, platforms, signals, masts — to allow safe vehicle passage under all operating conditions.

  • Static Vehicle Gauge — maximum envelope of a stationary vehicle
  • Dynamic Vehicle Gauge — envelope during motion (wider: sway, suspension travel, cant deficiency)
  • Structure Gauge — clearance envelope for fixed infrastructure = Dynamic gauge + safety margin
Engineering principle: The binding gauge for any route is the most restrictive gauge along that entire route. A wagon travelling from Edirne to Kayseri must comply with the most constrained tunnel or bridge it will pass through — it cannot be loaded to the dimensions of the wider sections.

‰ vs % — the unit that trips everyone up: Roads use percent (% = m rise per 100 m horizontal). Railways use per mille (‰ = m rise per 1,000 m horizontal) because railway gradients are typically 10× smaller. The TCDD mainline maximum of 8‰ = 0.8% — barely perceptible on foot, but significant when pushing a 2,000-tonne freight train.

Liberalisation (2013–2017): Law No. 6461 separated TCDD into an infrastructure manager (TCDD) and train operator (TCDD Taşımacılık). This creates new commercial and engineering challenges in capacity allocation, timetabling, and infrastructure pricing.

Despite the network expansion, Türkiye remains highway-oriented: 90.85% of passenger-km and 90.56% of freight ton-km by road in 2023. Shifting even 5% to rail would remove hundreds of thousands of trucks from Turkish roads — a major policy and engineering challenge for your generation.

01ConceptualThe Case for Rail in Türkiye▼

Türkiye's modal split shows railways carrying ~1.5% of passenger-km (2023). Germany manages ~8%, Switzerland ~18%, Japan ~29%. Identify and discuss three structural barriers — policy, investment history, geography, or demographics — that explain Turkey's low share, and for each barrier describe one engineering or policy measure that could address it.

What happened to railway investment after 1950? What is the relationship between the Marshall Plan and Turkey's highway network? What does the 2017 liberalisation change?
02QuantitativeGauge Interoperability▼

A freight train departs Köln, Germany (standard gauge 1,435 mm) bound for Moscow (Russian broad gauge 1,520 mm). At the Brest gauge-change facility (Belarus/Poland border):

(a) Calculate the gauge difference in mm and as a % of standard gauge.
(b) Explain two engineering solutions for handling the gauge break.
(c) If 40 wagons require bogie exchange and each wagon takes 4 minutes with 3 bogies changed simultaneously, estimate the total time penalty.

For (b): research variable-gauge axle technology (Talgo/SUW 2000) and dual-gauge track. For (c): wagons are processed sequentially, but each wagon's bogies are changed simultaneously.
03ConceptualGradient Calculations▼

A proposed railway mainline rises 12.6 m over a horizontal distance of 1.8 km between two stations.

(a) Express this gradient in ‰.
(b) Express it in %.
(c) Is this acceptable for a TCDD mainline? State the standard and justify.
(d) If not acceptable, what horizontal distance is needed for the same 12.6 m height gain at the TCDD maximum?
(e) What height gain occurs over 25 km at the maximum permissible gradient?

Gradient (‰) = (rise / horizontal distance) × 1000. Rearrange for (d). TCDD mainline maximum gradient = 8‰.
04ConceptualStructure Gauge — Why It Matters▼

A double-track railway tunnel is being designed. Dynamic vehicle gauge envelope: height 4,250 mm above rail, half-width 1,620 mm from each track centreline. Track centres: 4,000 mm. Structure gauge clearance: minimum 200 mm beyond dynamic envelope on all sides. Maintenance walkway: 500 mm along one wall.

(a) Calculate minimum internal tunnel width.
(b) Calculate minimum internal tunnel height from rail level.
(c) Sketch a dimensioned cross-section showing both tracks, all clearances, and the walkway.
(d) Explain why the dynamic gauge exceeds the static vehicle dimensions — name two physical causes.

Width = track centres + (clearance + half-vehicle-width) on each outer side + walkway. For (d): think about what the train does physically at speed — lateral oscillation, suspension compression under cornering loads.
